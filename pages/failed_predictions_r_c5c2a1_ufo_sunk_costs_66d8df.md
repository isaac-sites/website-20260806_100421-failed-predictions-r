## Introduction

When a dated UFO claim fails, the people who sacrificed most for it may face the greatest difficulty admitting that the prediction—and perhaps its source—was wrong. Money and [possessions]({{ 'possessions/' | relative_url }}) are only part of the investment. Believers may also have surrendered jobs, relationships, privacy, reputation and a former sense of identity. Those costs cannot be recovered simply by changing one’s mind.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/failed_predictions_r_c5c2a1_ufo_sunk_costs_66d8df-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Sunk Costs" width="1536" height="1024" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

This creates a powerful temptation to preserve the larger belief through reinterpretation. The spacecraft may be said to have arrived invisibly, postponed its landing because humanity was unready, or withheld contact after believers altered events spiritually. Remaining loyal can then protect more than a prediction: it can protect the meaning assigned to years of sacrifice.

Yet sunk costs do not make continued belief inevitable. Research on failed prophecy shows varied outcomes, including reinterpretation, quiet disengagement, factional disagreement and outright departure. The decisive issue is often whether members still possess a supportive community and a usable story that makes their earlier commitment appear worthwhile.

## Commitment before the deadline

A failed UFO prediction becomes especially difficult to confront when it has already shaped observable behaviour. Someone who merely read an online claim can leave it behind at little cost. Someone who publicly defended a contactee, travelled to a landing site, disposed of belongings or damaged family relationships has much more at stake.

The classic example is the small circle around [Dorothy Martin]({{ 'dorothy-martin/' | relative_url }}), who said that extraterrestrial beings would rescue selected people before a catastrophic flood on 21 December 1954. Accounts associated with the study *When Prophecy Fails* describe participants who altered work, study, relationships and possessions while preparing for the expected evacuation. Such actions made the prediction part of their public biography rather than a private speculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theatlantic.com/health/archive/2015/12/the-christmas-the-aliens-didnt-come/421122/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theatlantic.com">[The Atlantic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theatlantic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The AtlanticThe Christmas the Aliens Didn&#x27;t ComeDecember 18, 2015 — 18 Dec 2015 — It all started with a prophecy that a massive flood was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 18, 2015</span></span></span>

These sacrifices mattered psychologically because rejection of the prophecy would require several painful admissions at once. A believer might have to accept not only that the spacecraft failed to appear, but also that warnings from relatives were justified, that social losses were unnecessary and that confidence in the source had been misplaced. Remaining loyal allows those losses to be represented as principled sacrifices rather than avoidable mistakes.

Social psychologists use the term **sunk cost** for resources already spent and no longer recoverable. Rationally, such costs should not determine what a person believes or does next. In practice, previous investment can encourage an **escalation of commitment**: continuing along a failing path partly to avoid conceding that the original investment was wasted. Research describes self-justification, personal responsibility and social pressure as important contributors to this pattern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5904751/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)A New Inaction-Effect Perspective on the Sunk-Cost Fallacyby G Feldman · 2018 · Cited by 64 — Escalation-of-commitmen...</span></span></span>

The mechanism is broader than money. In UFO prophecy groups, four forms of investment can become entangled:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Material investment:** donations, travel, property, supplies or abandoned employment.
* **Reputational investment:** public statements, media appearances and arguments with sceptical friends.
* **Relational investment:** closer dependence on believers after distancing from outsiders.
* **Identity investment:** adopting a role such as contactee, chosen witness, spiritual messenger or member of an advanced minority.

</div>

The last two can be especially important. Losing money is painful, but abandoning a failed prophecy may also mean losing one’s closest relationships and the identity that organised daily life. The practical question after failure is therefore not simply, “Was the prediction accurate?” It may feel more like, “Who will I be, and who will still stand beside me, if I say it was wrong?”

Experimental research on effort and group attachment offers a related, though not UFO-specific, clue. In the well-known initiation study by Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills, participants who underwent a more embarrassing initiation subsequently rated an unimpressive group more favourably than participants who entered more easily. The finding does not prove that every severe sacrifice creates loyalty, but it illustrates how people may increase the value assigned to a group after paying an uncomfortable price to join it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Motivation/Aronson_Mills_1959_The_effect_of_severity_of_initiation.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: web.mit.edu">[Massachusetts Institute of Technology]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">web.mit.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Massachusetts Institute of TechnologyTHE EFFECT OF SEVERITY OF INITIATION ON LIKING...December 9, 2004 — by E ARONSON · Cited by 2496 —...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 9, 2004</span></span></span>

## How sunk costs shape interpretation

Sunk costs do not erase the failed deadline. They influence what members are motivated to do with it. The most useful reinterpretations preserve the value of previous sacrifice while changing the meaning of the missing event.

### Failure can become a test of worthiness

One response is to move the burden of explanation away from the source. Instead of concluding that the contactee was mistaken, members may decide that humanity was too violent, spiritually undeveloped or sceptical to receive extraterrestrial visitors. The prediction’s failure then confirms the group’s moral diagnosis of the outside world.

This move is particularly effective because it cannot be easily disproved. A visible landing was testable; a claim that unseen beings withdrew after detecting humanity’s “low vibration” is not. The believer’s earlier preparation can still be portrayed as correct, while outsiders become responsible for the disappointing outcome.

The Unarius Academy of Science provides a well-documented example. Its members anticipated a public arrival by benevolent “Space Brothers”, eventually associated with a fleet landing in 2001. Ethnographer Diana Tumminia found that members did not all hold the prediction in the same literal way. As the date approached, some suggested that human conflict—including the attacks of 11 September 2001—could prevent or delay the visitors’ arrival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academic.oup.com/book/7811/chapter/152993807" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academic.oup.com">[OUP Academic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academic.oup.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Academic9 Preparation for the Landing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academic9 Preparation for the Landing</span></span></span>

After no fleet appeared, the failure could therefore be absorbed into an existing worldview: extraterrestrial contact remained real, but terrestrial conditions were unsuitable. The absent ships did not necessarily force members to decide between total belief and total disbelief. They could downgrade the timetable while retaining the group’s teachings, practices and relationships.

### A physical event can be spiritualised

Another response is to claim that the promised event occurred on a plane inaccessible to ordinary observation. A landing becomes a spiritual contact; a rescue becomes an inner transformation; a public [revelation]({{ 'revelation/' | relative_url }}) becomes a shift in collective consciousness.

This protects investment by separating the truth of the teaching from the literal forecast that attracted attention. Members can say that outsiders misunderstood figurative language, or that only spiritually prepared people could recognise what happened. The deadline remains important, but its evidential standard changes after the fact.

Scholars of failed prophecy describe this as **spiritualisation**, one of several ways groups preserve a central belief while revising its failed empirical expression. It is not necessarily a calculated deception. Members may sincerely interpret ambiguous experiences—dreams, coincidences, feelings of energy or new messages—as evidence that something significant occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/1999-dawson.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gwern.net">[Gwern]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gwern.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When Prophecy Fails and Faith Persists</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When Prophecy Fails and Faith Persists</span></span></span>

### The sacrifice itself can be credited with averting disaster

A particularly strong reinterpretation presents non-occurrence as success. In the conventional account of the 1954 [Martin case]({{ 'martin-case/' | relative_url }}), a message received after the missed deadline stated that the group’s spiritual light had spared the world. The lack of a flood was thus reframed as evidence that the believers’ vigil and faith had worked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theatlantic.com/health/archive/2015/12/the-christmas-the-aliens-didnt-come/421122/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theatlantic.com">[The Atlantic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theatlantic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The AtlanticThe Christmas the Aliens Didn&#x27;t ComeDecember 18, 2015 — 18 Dec 2015 — It all started with a prophecy that a massive flood was...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 18, 2015</span></span></span>

This explanation converts sunk cost into causal power. Lost sleep, public ridicule and abandoned plans no longer look pointless; they become the very actions that saved others. It also makes sceptical criticism difficult, because the evidence of failure—the continued existence of the world—is claimed as evidence of success.

The logical problem is clear. Before the deadline, the predicted catastrophe and rescue were supposed to be observable. Afterwards, their absence was explained by an unannounced intervention that could not be independently checked. Nevertheless, the revised story serves an important emotional and social function: it restores purpose to sacrifices that would otherwise appear wasted.

### Loyalty to the source can be separated from the date

Believers may also preserve confidence in a contactee while treating the failed deadline as a minor transmission error. The source may have misunderstood symbolic information, confused earthly and extraterrestrial calendars, introduced personal emotions into a message or accurately perceived an event whose timing remained conditional.

[Ruth Norman]({{ 'ruth-norman/' | relative_url }}), a leader of Unarius, announced several expected extraterrestrial arrivals during the 1970s. When a landing did not occur, she attributed the mistaken forecast to unresolved trauma from a supposed past life. Some followers left, but the organisation continued, and the anticipated arrival was eventually shifted towards 2001.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Norman" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ruth Norman</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ruth Norman</span></span></span>

This form of reinterpretation concedes limited error while protecting charisma. The messenger may have been wrong about a date without being dismissed as a false source altogether. For long-standing members, that distinction is valuable: it permits correction without requiring the wholesale rejection of teachings, personal experiences and relationships accumulated around the leader.

## Why some members leave while others stay

The popular image of failed prophecy assumes one uniform reaction: either everyone wakes up or everyone doubles down. The evidence is more complicated. The same missed UFO deadline can produce firmer loyalty in one member, quiet withdrawal in another and selective reinterpretation in a third.

A major reason is that investments differ. A peripheral supporter who has lost little can revise their view without reorganising life. A central member may have built friendships, status and purpose around the claim. Leaving would impose new costs even after the prediction has failed.

**Members are more likely to remain when the group offers:**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a rapid explanation that preserves the larger worldview;
* strong agreement among trusted fellow believers;
* meaningful roles, routines and friendships unrelated to the deadline;
* a way to portray sacrifice as spiritually or historically important;
* a revised prediction that keeps fulfilment close enough to sustain hope.

</div>

**Members are more likely to leave when:**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the failed event was understood in strictly literal terms;
* leaders offer conflicting or obviously improvised explanations;
* the member retains supportive relationships outside the group;
* repeated postponements weaken trust rather than renewing anticipation;
* the social, financial or emotional cost of remaining exceeds the cost of departure.

</div>

Research on religious disaffiliation supports the importance of social context. Leaving a belief system can involve changes in relationships, identity and wellbeing, especially where religious or spiritual affiliation structures everyday social life. Disbelief and organisational departure also need not occur simultaneously: someone may privately doubt while remaining socially involved, or leave a group while retaining parts of its worldview.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4792192/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Leaving My Religion: Understanding the Relationship</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pub Med Central (PMC)Leaving My Religion: Understanding the Relationship</span></span></span>

Unarius again shows why a binary model is inadequate. Tumminia’s research found varied levels of literal expectation before the predicted 2001 landing. Some participants regarded the prophecy as central; others valued the organisation’s teachings, artistic productions, reincarnation narratives or therapeutic practices more than the timetable. When the ships failed to appear, members did not all lose the same thing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://brill.com/display/book/edcoll/9789004222687/B9789004222687-s010.pdf?srsltid=AfmBOor-YIhuYumKeJDdoHw_7nfOFuF-CZd-a6vHLtECIiS9oIDTvz0p" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: brill.com">[Brill]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">brill.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on brill.com.</span></span></span>

The group’s shared interpretive system also allowed uncertainty to be managed collectively. Members could connect the failed landing to humanity’s spiritual condition, past-life narratives or a longer cosmic process. Such explanations were sustained not simply by an isolated individual refusing evidence, but by conversations in which members made the disappointment intelligible to one another. Tumminia describes this as an intersubjective process: the prophecy’s meaning was produced and maintained socially.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/3712078" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jstor.org.</span></span></span>

Departures still occurred. Earlier missed Unarius predictions led some students to question Norman’s explanations, and leadership disputes later encouraged others to emphasise different parts of the tradition. The case therefore does not show that sunk costs trap every member permanently. It shows how an organisation can survive a failed forecast when it contains enough practices, relationships and flexible meanings to remain valuable after the deadline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Norman" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ruth Norman</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ruth Norman</span></span></span>

## The limits of the classic “doubling down” story

The 1954 Martin episode has often been presented as decisive proof that heavily committed believers respond to disconfirmation by becoming more zealous. That interpretation helped inspire cognitive [dissonance]({{ 'dissonance/' | relative_url }}) theory, but recent historical work makes the case less straightforward than popular retellings suggest.

Thomas Kelly’s archival reassessment argues that the investigators who infiltrated the group affected the events they claimed merely to observe. According to his findings, the group had already engaged in public outreach before the failed prediction, while some participants abandoned their beliefs rapidly afterwards. One investigator, Henry Riecken, later acknowledged helping to precipitate important events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41186060/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

These criticisms do not establish that sacrifice is irrelevant. They show that the Martin case should not be treated as a clean experiment proving a universal rule. Researchers and journalists formed a conspicuous presence around a very small circle, and behaviour interpreted as spontaneous proselytising may partly have reflected outside pressure or intervention.

There is also a broader **survivorship problem** in studies of failed prophecy. Groups that reinterpret failure and continue to exist leave organisations, publications and members available for researchers to study. Groups that dissolve quickly can disappear from the historical record. This may make belief persistence seem more common and more uniform than it really is. Kelly has argued that failed prophecies are often damaging or fatal to movements, contrary to the familiar claim that disconfirmation routinely strengthens them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journal.equinoxpub.com/IJSNR/article/view/33085" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journal.equinoxpub.com">[Equinox Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journal.equinoxpub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on equinoxpub.com.</span></span></span>

The safest conclusion is therefore conditional. Large sacrifices can increase pressure to defend a failed UFO claim, especially when loyal companions and flexible explanations remain available. But sacrifice can also intensify feelings of betrayal. A person who gave up a job or alienated family may become more loyal to justify the loss—or more hostile when the leader’s explanation no longer seems credible.

Sunk costs describe a pressure, not a destiny. Their effect depends on what happens around the disappointed believer: whether dissent is socially possible, whether outsiders offer support, whether the leader accepts any responsibility and whether the group still provides valued relationships or practices after its headline prediction has failed.

## The lasting lesson

Failed UFO predictions test more than belief in extraterrestrial contact. They test whether earlier sacrifices can be given a tolerable meaning. For highly committed members, saying “the spacecraft did not come” may be factually simple but personally enormous. It can threaten status, friendships, identity and the story that made painful choices seem worthwhile.

That is why the most durable post-failure explanations do not merely supply a new date. They rescue the value of commitment. Humanity was unready; the event occurred spiritually; believers prevented the disaster; the messenger received the truth imperfectly. Each explanation protects a different part of the investment.

Understanding this mechanism also explains why ridicule is often ineffective. Mockery raises the reputational cost of reversal and may push a believer towards the only community that still treats their sacrifice with respect. Evidence matters, but leaving becomes easier when a person can acknowledge error without losing every relationship and every source of meaning at once.

The key distinction is not between rational outsiders and irrational believers. It is between low-cost and high-cost revision. The deeper a failed UFO claim has entered a person’s social and moral life, the more support they may need to examine it honestly—and the more tempting it becomes to transform a missed deadline into another reason to remain loyal.

    var url = template.replace('{query}', encodeQuery(query || '')).replace('{query_plain}', String(query || ''));
    var joiner = url.indexOf('?') >= 0 ? '&' : '?';
    var params = [];
    if (cfg.mkrid) params.push('mkrid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.mkrid));
    if (cfg.campaign_id) params.push('campid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.campaign_id));
    params.push('mkevt=1');
    params.push('mkcid=1');
    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
