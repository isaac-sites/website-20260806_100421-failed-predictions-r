## Introduction

Claims that researchers may have fabricated or introduced purportedly psychic communications into [Dorothy Martin]({{ 'dorothy-martin/' | relative_url }})'s flying saucer group have become one of the most controversial aspects of the reassessment of *When Prophecy Fails*. If correct, the issue goes beyond ordinary observer influence. Instead of merely recording beliefs as they unfolded, investigators would have helped create some of the very evidence later interpreted as proof of believers' reactions to failed prophecy. That possibility matters because the [Martin case]({{ 'martin-case/' | relative_url }}) has long been treated as a classic example of how UFO groups respond when predictions fail. If communications presented as spontaneous revelations were instead researcher-generated, they blur the distinction between observing a movement and actively shaping its internal narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online Lib...</span></span></span>

## What the archives allege

The traditional account of *When Prophecy Fails* described researchers as covert participant-observers who entered Martin's circle posing as sincere believers while attempting to minimise their influence. More recent archival research argues that the intervention was considerably more active than the published book acknowledged. According to historian Thomas Kelly's analysis of previously unavailable archival material, researchers did not simply witness psychic communications but at times helped manufacture or encourage them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online Lib...</span></span></span>

## How planted messages could redirect belief

The significance of fabricated communications lies less in any single message than in the mechanism they create inside a small, high-commitment group.

Psychic circles often treated new messages as authoritative guidance from higher intelligences. If an apparently independent participant introduced a [revelation]({{ 'revelation/' | relative_url }}) supporting the group's expectations, members could interpret it as external confirmation rather than as another individual's opinion. That changes the informational environment in several ways.

First, it increases perceived consensus. Members may conclude that multiple independent channels are producing compatible revelations, strengthening confidence even if the apparent agreement originates from a single outside source.

Second, new communications can redirect interpretation after disappointment. Rather than allowing members to debate why a prophecy failed, a fresh message can offer an immediate explanation—for example, that higher beings altered their plans or that spiritual success occurred invisibly. If such explanations originate with investigators, it becomes difficult to determine whether believers developed them independently.

Third, fabricated revelations may reinforce leadership. Messages that appear to validate a leader's authority or previous predictions can reduce internal disagreement precisely when failed expectations might otherwise fragment the group.

Finally, the mere existence of additional revelations increases the apparent volume of supernatural evidence. In movements that place considerable weight on accumulated messages rather than empirical verification, quantity itself can become persuasive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online Lib...</span></span></span>

## The importance of Henry Riecken's reported role

One allegation receiving particular attention concerns psychologist Henry Riecken. Kelly argues from archival evidence that Riecken presented himself as possessing spiritual authority within the group and later acknowledged having "precipitated" crucial events during the study. If accurately interpreted, this represents a substantially more interventionist role than the detached observation described in the classic account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online Lib...</span></span></span>

This distinction is important because participant observation normally accepts some unavoidable influence while attempting to avoid directing the group's beliefs. Assuming a recognised spiritual role would instead position the researcher as someone capable of affecting doctrinal development from within.

Not all scholars have yet accepted Kelly's broader reinterpretation, and the long-standing psychological literature has been built upon the original publication for decades. Nevertheless, the newly published archival claims have shifted discussion from whether observers influenced behaviour to whether they sometimes helped generate the evidence itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online Lib...</span></span></span>

## Why manufactured evidence changes interpretation

If manufactured messages formed part of the group's internal communications, several conclusions traditionally drawn from the Martin case become less secure.

The first concerns cognitive [dissonance]({{ 'dissonance/' | relative_url }}). The famous interpretation held that believers spontaneously developed new explanations after prophecy failed. If researchers introduced or encouraged explanatory revelations, some observed rationalisations may no longer represent independent psychological adaptation.

The second concerns recruitment. Apparent confidence or renewed missionary activity following disappointment could partly reflect reactions to externally supplied encouragement rather than internally generated conviction.

The third concerns historical reconstruction. Later researchers relying on *When Prophecy Fails* generally assumed that recorded communications originated from the believers themselves. Fabricated material would complicate attempts to distinguish authentic group beliefs from products of the research process.

In methodological terms, the case shifts from illustrating failed-prophecy dynamics alone to illustrating how intervention can contaminate evidence in small social groups. In groups numbering only a handful of committed participants, even one apparently authoritative outsider may exert disproportionate influence over subsequent events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online Lib...</span></span></span>

## What remains established—and what remains disputed

Several points can now be separated more clearly.

It is well established that the researchers employed covert participant observation and concealed their identity from the group. It is also established that the Martin case became the foundation for one of psychology's best-known discussions of cognitive dissonance and failed prophecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_Prophecy_Fails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When Prophecy Fails</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When Prophecy Fails</span></span></span>

The newer and more controversial claims concern the extent of active intervention. Kelly's archival study argues that fabricated psychic messages, covert manipulation and researcher participation fundamentally altered the episode that later became famous. These claims have appeared in a peer-reviewed historical journal and are supported by archival documentation released through an accompanying research archive, but their broader implications are still being evaluated within the history of psychology and sociology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online Lib...</span></span></span>

For historians of UFO movements, the central lesson is methodological rather than paranormal.
