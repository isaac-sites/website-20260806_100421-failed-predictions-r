## Introduction

When documenting failed UFO predictions, conflicting dates are common rather than exceptional. A single prediction may display one date on a webpage, another in an internet archive, another in an email header, and yet another in a newspaper report. These dates are not necessarily contradictory because they measure different stages in the life of the same claim. The key question is not which date is "correct", but what each timestamp proves. A reliable chronology distinguishes when a prediction was written, when it became publicly accessible, when independent evidence shows it existed, and when the predicted event was supposed to occur. Keeping these separate prevents both unfair criticism and unjustified rehabilitation of predictions after their deadlines have passed.

## Which Timestamp Should You Trust?

The safest approach is to treat each timestamp as evidence for a different historical fact instead of searching for a single authoritative date.

A well-documented UFO prediction should record at least three distinct dates:

Date typeWhat it provesCommon source**Claim creation date**When the author says the prediction was written or sentPage footer, document metadata, email "Date" field**Independent existence date**The earliest externally verifiable evidence that the wording existedWeb archive capture, newspaper publication, mailing-list archive, library copy**Prediction deadline**When the promised UFO event was expected to occurThe prediction itself

These dates answer different historical questions. An author's stated publication date may indicate intent, but an independent archive demonstrates that the wording actually existed before the deadline. Likewise, a prediction's deadline determines whether later revisions should be considered part of the original claim or post-event interpretation.

For documenting [failed predictions]({{ 'failed-predictions-relating-to-ufos/' | relative_url }}), the most important relationship is:

> **Independent evidence must pre-date the prediction deadline.**

If the only surviving copy appeared after the event supposedly occurred, it becomes much harder to establish exactly what was originally promised.

## The Three Dates Every Record Needs

A complete chronology becomes much easier to interpret when each record separates the following elements.

### Visible publication date

This is the date shown to readers on a webpage, blog post, newsletter or printed document.

It is useful but not decisive. Website owners can edit displayed dates, migrate content to new publishing systems or republish older material under newer timestamps. Conversely, some websites deliberately preserve an original publication date even after substantial revisions.

The visible date therefore documents how the publisher presents the material, not necessarily when the specific wording first became public.

### Independent capture date

This is often the strongest evidence.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Internet Archive Wayback Machine captures.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://archivesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360004651732-Using-The-Wayback-Machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archivesupport.zendesk.com">[archivesupport.zendesk.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archivesupport.zendesk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Help CenterUsing The Wayback Machine – Internet Archive Help CenterJanuary 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 13, 2021</span></span></span>
* National web archives.
* Archived mailing-list messages.
* Newspaper publication dates.
* Library deposit records.
* Contemporary forum quotations.

</div>

An archived capture proves only that the page existed **no later than** the capture time. It does **not** prove that the page first appeared on that date. A page could have been online for weeks before anyone archived it. The Internet Archive itself notes that "Save Page Now" creates a one-time archived capture rather than establishing when a page originally went live.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archivesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360004651732-Using-The-Wayback-Machine" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archivesupport.zendesk.com">[Internet Archive Help Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archivesupport.zendesk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive Help CenterUsing The Wayback Machine – Internet Archive Help CenterJanuary 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 13, 2021</span></span></span>

Consequently, the earliest archive capture should be treated as the latest confirmed point by which the wording existed, not necessarily its moment of publication.

### Event deadline

This is the promised date for the UFO event itself.

Unlike publication dates, this usually comes directly from the claimant's wording:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* "Ships will appear on 14 October."
* "Disclosure will occur before Christmas."
* "Landings will begin within three days."

</div>

Everything else in the chronology should be interpreted relative to this deadline.

## Why Visible Publication Dates Can Mislead

Conflicting dates often arise because modern publishing systems rewrite metadata while leaving the text largely unchanged.

Common causes include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* website redesigns that assign new publication dates;
* migration between blogging platforms;
* corrected typographical errors;
* updated image galleries;
* new categories or tags;
* automatic CMS (content management system) updates.

</div>

A prediction that originally appeared in 2007 may therefore display a 2012 publication date after a site rebuild even though its wording remained largely unchanged.

The reverse problem also occurs.

Some authors edit predictions after a failed deadline but preserve the original publication date. Without archived versions, later readers may wrongly assume the softened wording existed from the beginning.

This is why historical comparison between versions matters more than relying on a single displayed date.

## Reading Email Dates Correctly

Email records introduce another layer of timestamps.

Several dates can legitimately appear in one message:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the author's "Date" header;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://archivesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360004716091-Wayback-Machine-General-Information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archivesupport.zendesk.com">[archivesupport.zendesk.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archivesupport.zendesk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">360004716091 Wayback Machine General Information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">360004716091 Wayback Machine General Information</span></span></span>
* the sequence of "Received" timestamps added by mail servers;
* archive ingestion dates for mailing lists;
* local download dates in recipients' inboxes.

</div>

Under the Internet Message Format standard, the **Date** header records when the sender completed the message for transmission, while the **Received** headers record its progress through mail servers. These timestamps answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rfc-editor.org/info/rfc5322/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rfc-editor.org">[RFC Editor]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rfc-editor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RFC EditorRFC 5322: Internet Message Format &#124; RFC Editor...</span></span></span>

For UFO [prediction records]({{ 'prediction-records/' | relative_url }}), this distinction matters because:

* the author's Date header indicates when the message was reportedly sent;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://archivesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360004716091-Wayback-Machine-General-Information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archivesupport.zendesk.com">[archivesupport.zendesk.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archivesupport.zendesk.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">360004716091 Wayback Machine General Information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">360004716091 Wayback Machine General Information</span></span></span>
* server routing records independently demonstrate that the email travelled through mail systems before the prediction deadline.

Multiple recipients with matching copies provide stronger corroboration than a single forwarded message discovered years later.

## Resolving Conflicts Across Archives, Email and Print

When dates disagree, reconstruct the chronology instead of choosing one source.

A practical order of evaluation is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Identify the prediction deadline.
2. Locate the earliest independently verifiable copy.
3. Compare that wording with later versions.
4. Record every significant timestamp separately.
5. Explain what each timestamp proves.

</div>

Consider a hypothetical example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Website displays: **5 March**
* Wayback capture: **28 April**
* Newspaper quotes prediction: **18 March**
* Event deadline: **1 May**

</div>

This does **not** mean the website date is false.

Instead, the evidence shows:

* the author claims publication on 5 March;
* independent press coverage confirms the prediction existed by 18 March;
* the archived webpage confirms it certainly existed by 28 April;
* all verified evidence predates the 1 May deadline.

That produces a coherent chronology without requiring every source to match exactly.

## When Printed Sources Outweigh Digital Ones

Printed material often resolves online uncertainty.

A dated newspaper, magazine, conference programme or printed leaflet can demonstrate that a prediction circulated publicly before later website edits occurred.

Print evidence is particularly valuable when:

* the original website has disappeared;
* archived captures begin only after revisions;
* domain ownership has changed;
* online timestamps conflict.

Library copies, newspaper databases and scanned periodicals can therefore provide an independent anchor for dating UFO predictions.

## Building a Reliable Chronology

The strongest documentation avoids collapsing multiple timestamps into a single "publication date". Instead, every important record should explicitly distinguish:

* **author's stated publication date**;
* **earliest independently verified existence**;
* **prediction deadline**;
* **later revisions or explanations**, each with their own dates.

This approach preserves historical accuracy without assuming that conflicting timestamps indicate deception. In many cases they simply reflect different stages of publication, preservation and circulation. Only by separating these functions can researchers fairly determine whether a UFO prediction genuinely existed before its promised event and whether later alterations changed the claim after the deadline had already passed.
