## Introduction

When assessing [failed predictions relating to UFOs]({{ 'failed-predictions-relating-to-ufos/' | relative_url }}), the decisive issue is often not whether something unusual happened, but whether the original wording committed the claimant to an outcome that could clearly succeed or fail. Small differences in language determine whether a prediction is genuinely testable or can be reinterpreted after the deadline. A statement promising that "a craft will appear over London on 14 October at sunset and remain visible for three hours" creates an objective public test. By contrast, a statement that "many people may sense increased extraterrestrial activity around mid-October" does not, because no agreed observation could prove it wrong.

This distinction matters when documenting predictions before the deadline. Preserving the exact wording allows later readers to judge whether the prediction actually failed, rather than relying on retrospective summaries or revised interpretations.

## The Commitments Hidden Inside Prediction Wording

A prediction becomes testable only when its language commits the speaker to observable facts. Every additional detail reduces the range of possible reinterpretations.

The most important commitments are:

* **A definite time** rather than an open-ended period.
* **A specific location** or clearly defined area.
* **An observable event** that independent witnesses could recognise.
* **An identifiable audience**, such as "visible to everyone" rather than "visible only to the spiritually prepared."
* **A measurable duration** where relevant.
* **An outcome that can plainly occur or fail to occur.**

These features mirror the broader idea of [falsifiability]({{ 'falsifiability/' | relative_url }}) in the philosophy of science. A claim becomes genuinely testable when it is possible to specify observations that would contradict it rather than merely support it. Popper described empirical "basic statements" as observations occurring at identifiable places and times that can serve as tests of broader claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iep.utm.edu/pop-sci/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iep.utm.edu">[Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iep.utm.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on utm.edu.</span></span></span>

For UFO predictions, the principle is practical rather than philosophical: if nobody can describe in advance what would count as failure, the prediction cannot later be evaluated fairly.

## Certain Claims Versus Vague Possibilities

The wording itself often reveals whether the author is making a commitment or merely suggesting a possibility.

A strong, testable prediction typically uses language such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* "will appear"
* "will land"
* "will be visible"
* "will occur on"
* "everyone will witness"

</div>

These phrases promise an event that either happens or does not.

By contrast, weaker formulations commonly include expressions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* "may appear"
* "could happen"
* "might become apparent"
* "energy will increase"
* "those who are receptive will notice"
* "contact may occur on a higher level"

</div>

These statements provide no single observation whose absence would clearly demonstrate failure. Because almost any later experience can be presented as confirmation, they remain difficult or impossible to test objectively.

The distinction is not that cautious wording is automatically wrong. Rather, cautious wording often reduces the predictive content. A forecast expressed only as a possibility makes far fewer commitments than one expressed as a certainty.

## How Escape Clauses Change the Test

One of the most important features to document is the presence of escape clauses.

An escape clause is wording that allows a prediction to survive almost any outcome by introducing conditions that become relevant only after the deadline.

Common examples include statements that:

* humanity was "[not ready]({{ 'not-ready/' | relative_url }})";

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* extraterrestrials changed their plans;
* the event occurred in another dimension;
* only spiritually advanced observers could perceive it;
* the timeline shifted;
* negative human thoughts prevented manifestation.

</div>

Such conditions may genuinely reflect the claimant's beliefs, but they fundamentally change how the prediction should be evaluated. Instead of promising a public event, they introduce additional assumptions that can prevent any clear disconfirmation.

For this reason, documenting the original wording before the predicted date is essential. If these conditions already appeared in the original text, they form part of the prediction and should be recorded accurately. If they appear only after the event failed to occur, they represent a later explanation rather than part of the original claim.

## Why Single Words Can Decide Whether a Prediction Failed

Minor wording differences can completely alter whether a missed prediction counts as a genuine failure.

Consider these examples:

Original wordingTestability"A spacecraft **will appear** over New York on 1 July."Clearly testable."A spacecraft **may appear** around early July."Considerably weaker."Many people **will feel** the presence of visitors."Difficult to test objectively."Visitors **will reveal themselves to world governments**."Publicly testable."Disclosure **has already begun energetically**."Effectively immune to straightforward testing.

The differences appear slight, yet each changes what evidence could demonstrate success or failure.

## A Practical Example

Blossom Goodchild's widely discussed 2008 prediction illustrates why preserving exact wording matters. The published prediction specified a visible appearance of a Federation of Light craft on **14 October 2008**, lasting **three days** and intended to demonstrate the existence of other life. Those concrete commitments—date, visibility and duration—created a prediction that could be evaluated against public observation. Later explanations issued after the event can therefore be distinguished from the original forecast because the original wording was publicly preserved before the deadline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiability" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

The lesson is not about whether particular beliefs are true or false. Rather, it shows that precise wording allows later discussion to focus on what was actually promised instead of on retrospective paraphrases.

## Recording the Exact Language Fairly

When documenting a UFO prediction for later evaluation, the safest practice is to preserve the claimant's own words before extracting any summary.

A reliable record should capture:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the exact quoted prediction;
* the publication date;
* every stated deadline;
* any named locations;
* who was expected to observe the event;
* whether the wording expressed certainty or possibility;
* every condition or qualification already present in the original text.

</div>

Only after preserving those details should researchers summarise the prediction. This prevents later disputes over whether critics exaggerated the claim or supporters softened it after the fact.

In practice, the exact wording is often the most important piece of evidence. The difference between a failed prediction and an untestable claim frequently lies not in the event itself, but in the precise words chosen before the deadline.
