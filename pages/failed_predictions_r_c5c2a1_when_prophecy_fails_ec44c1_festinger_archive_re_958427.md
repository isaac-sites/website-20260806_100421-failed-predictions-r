## Introduction

The reopening of Leon Festinger's sealed research papers has transformed a long-running debate over *When Prophecy Fails* (1956), the famous study of [Dorothy Martin]({{ 'dorothy-martin/' | relative_url }})'s UFO prophecy group. Earlier critics largely had to rely on the published book, scattered correspondence and recollections. Newly accessible notes, telephone transcripts, memoranda and related archival records now allow researchers to compare what the investigators privately recorded with what they later presented in print. That comparison has become central to the evidence dispute surrounding one of the best-known examples of a failed UFO prediction. Recent archival research argues that the surviving records challenge several of the book's most influential claims, while also exposing important methodological and ethical questions about the conduct of the field study itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online Lib...</span></span></span>

## What survives in the Festinger papers

The archival record is substantial but not complete. Materials now available include handwritten notes, internal memoranda, correspondence between the researchers, transcribed telephone conversations, observational records and related documents preserved principally through university archival collections. According to the recent archival analysis by Thomas Kelly, these sources have been supplemented by materials from the Gray Barker UFO Collection and have been organised in a publicly documented research dataset, allowing other scholars to examine many of the same documents independently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1002%2Fjhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online LibraryNovember 4, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 4, 2025</span></span></span>

These papers matter because they capture the research process before publication. Rather than relying solely on the polished narrative of *When Prophecy Fails*, historians can now examine contemporaneous notes describing what participants reportedly did, what the researchers believed they were observing, and how they discussed the project privately.

The surviving archive does not provide an uninterrupted record of every interaction. Like most historical collections, it contains gaps, uneven documentation and records created for different purposes. Nevertheless, it is extensive enough to permit detailed comparisons between private documentation and the published account.

## Which published claims can the archive test?

The newly opened records do not determine whether cognitive [dissonance]({{ 'dissonance/' | relative_url }}) exists as a psychological process. Instead, they address a narrower historical question: whether the [Martin case]({{ 'martin-case/' | relative_url }}) unfolded as described in *When Prophecy Fails*.

Several influential claims can now be compared directly with archival evidence.

**The timing of proselytising.** The published book presents public evangelism as a response to the failed prophecy. Kelly argues that the archival record instead shows members actively seeking publicity before the prediction failed, weakening the claim that missionary activity emerged primarily because of disconfirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1002%2Fjhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online LibraryNovember 4, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 4, 2025</span></span></span>

**What happened after the prophecy failed.** The classic narrative portrays a group that strengthened its commitment after disappointment. Kelly argues that private records instead document rapid withdrawal by key members, Dorothy Martin's recantation and the effective dissolution of the movement rather than sustained expansion. If correct, that would significantly alter the historical interpretation of the case while leaving broader theories of belief revision to be tested elsewhere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1002%2Fjhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online LibraryNovember 4, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 4, 2025</span></span></span>

**Researcher involvement.** One of the most striking aspects of the archive concerns the degree of interaction between observers and participants. The published study generally presents the researchers as covert observers. The archival materials have been interpreted as showing considerably more intervention, including active participation in group events and discussions about influencing unfolding situations. Kelly argues that one researcher later acknowledged having "precipitated" important events in the study, raising questions about whether the observed behaviour was entirely spontaneous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://doi.org/10.1002%2Fjhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: doi.org">[DOI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">doi.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online LibraryNovember 4, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 4, 2025</span></span></span>

### Comparing the public and private records

The archival evidence is especially valuable because it allows direct comparison rather than speculation.

Instead of asking whether critics have plausible alternative interpretations, historians can examine whether field notes, correspondence and transcripts support the chronology presented in the published book. This shifts the debate from broad theoretical disagreement to documentary consistency.

That distinction is important. A theory may remain useful even if one famous case study proves less reliable than previously believed. Conversely, an influential publication can accurately describe some events while overstating others. The archive allows individual claims to be evaluated separately rather than accepted or rejected as a package.

## What the records do not settle

Although the archive has significantly expanded the available evidence, it has not eliminated every uncertainty.

First, the surviving documents are incomplete. Not every conversation, observation or decision was preserved, and historians cannot assume that missing records would necessarily support one interpretation over another.

Second, much of the recent reassessment depends on how researchers interpret handwritten notes, private correspondence and observational records. Even where documents are authentic, reasonable disagreement may remain over what particular passages imply about motivation, intent or chronology.

Third, the archival findings concern one specific field study rather than cognitive dissonance theory as a whole. The documents may challenge whether the Martin case provides the clean demonstration described in *When Prophecy Fails*, but they do not by themselves invalidate decades of subsequent experimental research on belief change and dissonance. <div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Within the history of failed UFO predictions, the Festinger archive has changed the nature of the debate. Earlier discussions often centred on whether critics believed the published narrative. The newly available records instead allow many factual questions to be checked against contemporaneous documentation.

The principal historical contribution of the archive is therefore evidential rather than theoretical. It provides a richer documentary record of how researchers observed, interpreted and reported Dorothy Martin's 1954 UFO prophecy group. Whether scholars ultimately accept all of Kelly's conclusions or not, the newly opened papers mean that the famous case can no longer be evaluated solely through the published version of *When Prophecy Fails*.
