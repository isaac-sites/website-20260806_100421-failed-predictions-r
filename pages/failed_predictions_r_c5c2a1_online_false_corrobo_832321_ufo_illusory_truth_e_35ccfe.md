## Introduction

Repeated UFO predictions can begin to *feel* more credible even when no new evidence has appeared. This is not because the prediction has become more accurate, but because repeated exposure changes how people judge information. Psychologists call this the **illusory truth effect**: statements encountered before are processed more fluently, and that feeling of familiarity is often misinterpreted as a sign of truth. The mechanism helps explain why failed UFO forecasts may continue attracting belief long after they have been disproved. Within online communities, repeated reposts, reaction videos, summaries and debates can create the impression that a prediction has gained support, when in reality only its visibility has increased.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352250X23001811" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect: A review of how repetition increases belief in misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## How familiarity affects truth judgments

The illusory truth effect is one of the most consistently replicated findings in cognitive psychology. Rather than carefully evaluating every claim from scratch, people often use mental shortcuts. One of those shortcuts is **processing fluency**—the ease with which information is understood. Familiar statements require less mental effort to process, and that ease can be unconsciously interpreted as evidence that the statement is probably true.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8116821/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)The effects of repetition frequency on the illusory truth effect - PMCMay 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2021</span></span></span>

For failed UFO predictions, this distinction matters because repetition usually changes **recognition**, not **evidence**. Consider the typical online cycle:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A prediction is published by a single source.
* It is reposted across forums and social media.
* Videos discuss the prediction.
* News articles report that the prediction is circulating.
* Later discussions cite the widespread attention as if it reflects growing support.

</div>

At every stage, the prediction becomes more familiar, but its factual basis may remain identical to the original unsupported claim. Familiarity can therefore substitute for genuine corroboration in people's intuitive judgments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352250X23001811" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect: A review of how repetition increases belief in misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Importantly, this mechanism does not require gullibility or poor reasoning. The effect has been observed even when participants know that repeated information may be unreliable and even when they possess relevant background knowledge. Repetition alters the subjective experience of evaluating a statement before conscious reasoning begins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352250X23001811" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect: A review of how repetition increases belief in misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## Why one repetition can change sharing behaviour

Repetition influences more than belief—it can also influence whether people choose to pass information on.

Experimental research has shown that **even a single previous exposure** to misinformation makes people more likely to judge it as accurate and, in turn, more likely to share it on social media. Researchers found that increased perceived accuracy mediated the increase in sharing intentions. In other words, people were not simply sharing familiar claims because they recognised them; familiarity subtly made the claims appear more credible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10636596/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)The illusory truth effect leads to the spread of misinformation - PMC...</span></span></span>

This finding helps explain why unsuccessful UFO predictions can continue circulating after their predicted dates have passed.

A common sequence is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. A prediction receives extensive attention before the promised event.
2. The prediction fails.
3. Discussions continue explaining, reinterpreting or debating the failure.
4. New audiences encounter the original prediction through these later conversations.
5. The claim becomes increasingly familiar despite its failed outcome.

</div>

The repetition itself can therefore sustain visibility independently of evidential strength. The online discussion may become larger precisely because the prediction failed dramatically, exposing still more people to the original claim.

## Why failed predictions can remain persuasive

[Failed predictions]({{ 'failed-predictions-relating-to-ufos/' | relative_url }}) do not always disappear because memory is selective. Many readers remember encountering the prediction but only vaguely remember whether it succeeded, failed or remained unresolved. When the claim later reappears, the familiarity is immediate while the detailed context is often weaker.

For UFO predictions, repeated exposure can produce several misleading impressions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **"I've heard this before."** Familiarity is mistaken for reliability.
* **"Lots of people were talking about it."** Widespread discussion is confused with widespread evidence.
* **"There must be something behind it."** Repetition is interpreted as independent confirmation rather than repeated reporting of the same original claim.

</div>

These impressions arise without requiring deliberate deception. Much of the repetition comes from sincere discussion, speculation, criticism or entertainment. The psychological effect depends on repeated exposure, not on whether each repetition endorses the claim.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352250X23001811" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect: A review of how repetition increases belief in misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

## Limits of the illusory truth effect

The illusory truth effect is influential, but it is not irresistible.

People do not automatically believe every repeated claim. Several factors can reduce or override the effect:

* **Strong contradictory knowledge.** Well-established facts make repetition less persuasive, although studies show repetition can still exert some influence even when claims contradict existing knowledge.
* **Active verification.** Checking [original sources]({{ 'original-sources/' | relative_url }}), dates and outcomes shifts attention from familiarity to evidence.
* **Independent corroboration.** Multiple genuinely independent observations carry far greater evidential value than repeated references to the same prediction.
* **Clear memory of failure.** When audiences explicitly recall that a predicted UFO event did not occur, familiarity alone is less likely to restore credibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352250X23001811" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect: A review of how repetition increases belief in misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Research also suggests diminishing returns. Additional repetitions continue increasing perceived truth, but the largest jump occurs early. One experimental programme found that the greatest increase in perceived truth came when participants encountered a statement for the **second time**, with later repetitions producing progressively smaller gains. This helps explain why a prediction can acquire an initial aura of plausibility surprisingly quickly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8116821/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)The effects of repetition frequency on the illusory truth effect - PMCMay 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 13, 2021</span></span></span>

## Why repetition should not be mistaken for evidence

Within discussions of failed UFO predictions, the key lesson is that **visibility and evidential support are different things**. A prediction repeated across hundreds of posts is still supported only by whatever evidence existed before the first repost.

The illusory truth effect explains why repeated exposure can make unsupported forecasts feel increasingly plausible without changing their factual status. Understanding this mechanism helps distinguish two very different questions:

* **How many times has this prediction been repeated?**
* **What new independent evidence has actually emerged since it was first made?**

Only the second question addresses whether the prediction has gained factual support. The first primarily measures familiarity, and familiarity alone is not evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352250X23001811" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect: A review of how repetition increases belief in misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>
