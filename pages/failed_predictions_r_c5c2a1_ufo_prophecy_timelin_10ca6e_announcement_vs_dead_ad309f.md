## Introduction

When building a timeline of failed UFO predictions, every entry should contain **two separate dates**: the date the claim was publicly announced and the date the promised event was supposed to occur. Keeping those dates distinct is not a clerical detail. It reveals how long a prediction circulated, how much time it had to attract followers, media attention or financial commitment, and whether later accounts accurately describe the original sequence of events. If those dates are merged into a single timeline marker, hindsight compresses the history and obscures how belief developed before the prediction could be tested.

## The Difference Between an Issue Date and a Deadline

An **announcement date** marks the beginning of a prediction's public life. It records when a claimant first stated that a specific extraterrestrial event, landing, rescue or disclosure would occur.

An **event deadline** marks the point at which the claim became testable. Only after that date can the prediction be evaluated against observable reality.

These dates answer different historical questions.

* **Announcement date:** When did people first hear the claim?
* **Deadline:** When could observers determine whether the claim succeeded or failed?<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jhbs.70043?campaign=woletoc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[onlinelibrary.wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026 - Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences - Wiley Online LibraryNovember 4, 2025 — Volume...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 4, 2025</span></span></span>

Keeping both dates makes it possible to calculate the prediction's active lifespan. A prophecy announced years before its deadline represents a much longer period of influence than one issued only days beforehand, even if both ultimately failed.

For UFO prediction research, this distinction also preserves the original wording before later reinterpretations, revised calendars or retrospective editing alter the narrative.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_Prophecy_Fails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When Prophecy Fails</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When Prophecy Fails</span></span></span>

## How Publicity Grows During the Waiting Period

The period between announcement and deadline is often the most historically significant part of a failed UFO prediction.

During this interval, several measurable developments can occur:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* media coverage increases;
* followers recruit new believers;
* public meetings or lectures are organised;
* money or property may be committed;
* expectations become increasingly specific;
* sceptics begin documenting the prediction before the deadline.

</div>

Without recording the announcement date, these developments disappear from the historical record.

The well-known 1954 [Dorothy Martin]({{ 'dorothy-martin/' | relative_url }}) case illustrates this point. The prediction of catastrophic flooding and rescue by flying saucers circulated before the promised date of 21 December 1954, allowing believers to organise around it and researchers to observe the group before the prophecy reached its test. Whether one follows the traditional interpretation presented in *When Prophecy Fails* or more recent archival reassessments challenging aspects of that narrative, the chronology only makes sense because the prediction existed publicly before its deadline.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_Prophecy_Fails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When Prophecy Fails</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When Prophecy Fails</span></span></span>

Likewise, later UFO-related predictions, including highly publicised channelled announcements such as Blossom Goodchild's expected appearance of a Federation of Light spacecraft on 14 October 2008, generated discussion, anticipation and media attention for weeks before the promised date arrived. Recording only the failed deadline would erase that period of public expectation and make the event appear to have emerged suddenly rather than developing over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_dates_predicted_for_apocalyptic_events" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">List of dates predicted for apocalyptic events</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">List of dates predicted for apocalyptic events</span></span></span>

## Why Two Dates Improve Historical Comparisons

Maintaining separate announcement and deadline fields allows different UFO predictions to be compared fairly.

For example, two [failed predictions]({{ 'failed-predictions-relating-to-ufos/' | relative_url }}) might share the same promised event date but differ dramatically in their history:

Without the announcement date, both would appear equally important because only the failure date survives.

This distinction also allows historians to identify changing communication patterns. Earlier contactee movements often spread predictions through newsletters, meetings and printed books over many months, whereas modern internet claims can reach worldwide audiences almost instantly while still retaining a clearly identifiable publication date.

## Common Timeline Errors Caused by Date Compression

Combining announcement and deadline into a single date creates several recurring historical mistakes.

### It makes predictions appear shorter-lived

If only the deadline is recorded, readers may assume a prediction emerged shortly before it failed. In reality, some UFO promises circulated for months or years, shaping communities long before they were disproved.

### It hides changing expectations

Many predictions evolve between announcement and deadline. Dates may be refined, locations altered or additional promises added. Without the original issue date, these revisions become difficult to reconstruct.

### It obscures evidence created before failure

Newspaper reports, newsletters, recordings and eyewitness accounts produced before the deadline provide especially valuable evidence because they cannot be influenced by hindsight. Compressing the timeline encourages later summaries to replace these contemporaneous records.

After a failed prediction, claimants sometimes redefine what the original promise meant. If historians begin their timeline only at the missed deadline, these later explanations can inadvertently become merged with the initial claim instead of being recognised as separate stages in the story.

## Preserving Cause Before Outcome

A chronological timeline should always show cause before consequence.

The sequence ideally runs as follows:

1. The prediction is announced.
2. It circulates publicly.
3. Followers respond.
4. Independent observers document the claim.
5. The deadline arrives.
6. The observable outcome is recorded.
7. Any reinterpretations or revised explanations are logged separately.

This ordering prevents later narratives from reshaping earlier events. It also makes it easier to distinguish between what was originally promised and what believers said after the promised event failed to occur.

## Why Every UFO Promise Needs Two Dates

For historians, journalists and researchers studying failed UFO predictions, separating announcement dates from event deadlines is a simple but powerful safeguard against hindsight bias. The announcement date records when a claim entered public discussion, while the deadline records when reality could test it. Between those points lies the period in which expectations spread, commitments were made and evidence accumulated.

A timeline that preserves both dates therefore documents not only **whether** a UFO prediction failed, but also **how long it influenced people before it could be judged**, providing a clearer and more accurate historical record than a single-date chronology ever can.
