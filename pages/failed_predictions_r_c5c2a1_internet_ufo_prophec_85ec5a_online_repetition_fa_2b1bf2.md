## Introduction

One of the most influential features of internet-era UFO prophecies was not the accuracy of the predictions but the way identical claims appeared to gain credibility through repetition. A single prophecy could be copied across personal websites, discussion forums, email lists, blogs and later social media, creating the impression that many independent sources were reporting the same event. In reality, dozens of posts often traced back to one original message or channelled statement.

This mechanism mattered because readers frequently encountered the claim without seeing its origin. The growing number of copies made a prediction feel widely supported even when no new evidence had emerged. Within the history of failed UFO predictions, this process helps explain why some deadlines attracted large international audiences despite relying on a single unverified source.

Each repost usually retained the central prediction while omitting much of the surrounding context. Attribution often became weaker with every copy. Readers encountering the fifth or tenth version might have no indication that every article ultimately originated from one individual or one small group.

The Ground Crew movement studied by Christopher Helland and Susan Palmer illustrates how internet communities could circulate identical prophetic material among geographically dispersed participants. Their research shows that online interaction enabled believers to reinforce shared expectations even when they had never met in person. Rather than generating independent testimony, many participants were continually referring back to the same digital texts and discussions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352250X23001811" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect: A review of how repetition increases belief in misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

This pattern became increasingly visible during major internet-based UFO predictions, including the anticipation surrounding Blossom Goodchild's announced worldwide UFO appearance in October 2008. Copies of interviews, summaries and countdown posts spread rapidly across websites and forums, giving the impression of a broad information network even though the prediction itself originated from a single claimed channelled communication.

## Why Repetition Can Resemble Independent Evidence

People often use repetition as a practical shortcut when judging reliability. If multiple newspapers independently report the same event, the overlap usually increases confidence because the reports stem from separate investigations.

Online UFO prophecies frequently mimicked this appearance without providing genuine corroboration.

Instead of multiple independent witnesses, readers often saw:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the same wording copied repeatedly;
* quotations reproduced without checking the original publication;
* forum users citing websites that themselves had copied another website;
* email chains repeating claims without identifying the initial source.

</div>

The result was a form of false corroboration: apparent agreement created by duplication rather than independent observation.

Psychological research helps explain why this was persuasive. Decades of experiments on the **[illusory truth]({{ 'illusory-truth/' | relative_url }}) effect** show that repeated exposure alone makes statements feel more believable, even when repetition provides no new evidence. Familiarity increases processing fluency—the ease with which information is understood—and people can unconsciously mistake that familiarity for evidence of accuracy. Recent reviews demonstrate that this effect extends beyond trivia to misinformation and conspiracy-related claims shared online.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352250X23001811" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect: A review of how repetition increases belief in misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

Importantly, the mechanism does not require deliberate deception. Even sincere believers who repeatedly reposted the same prediction contributed to making it appear more widely supported than it actually was.

## Why Internet Architecture Amplified the Effect

Several features of early online communication strengthened this illusion.

**Search engines rewarded repetition.** Similar pages appearing across many websites increased the apparent visibility of a prediction, making it easier for users to encounter multiple copies during a search.

**Forums mixed original discussion with copied material.** A lengthy thread might contain dozens of replies quoting the same prophecy, making participation itself appear to validate the claim.

**Email forwarding removed context.** As messages passed between mailing lists, references to the original author often disappeared, leaving only the prediction itself.

**Archived copies extended the claim's lifespan.** Mirror sites and cached pages preserved messages long after the original website had changed or disappeared, allowing old prophecies to continue circulating as if they were current.

These features produced an information environment in which volume could be mistaken for verification.

## Ways to Trace a Claim Back to Its Source

Because repetition creates only the appearance of multiple witnesses, evaluating a UFO prediction requires tracing the information backwards rather than counting how many websites mention it.

Useful checks include:

* **Locate the earliest available publication.** Archived versions of websites can reveal whether later articles simply copied an earlier post.
* **Compare wording.** Identical phrases across many sites usually indicate copying rather than independent reporting.
* **Separate commentary from evidence.** Hundreds of discussions about a prediction do not constitute confirmation of the prediction itself.
* **Look for genuinely independent sources.** Independent corroboration requires separate observations or evidence, not repeated quotations from the same claimant.
* **Check whether later articles add verifiable information.** If every article ultimately cites the same channelled message, the apparent number of sources is misleading.

This approach shifts attention from popularity to provenance—where the claim actually originated.

## Why Failed UFO Predictions Often Appeared Better Supported Than They Were

Many internet UFO prophecies gained momentum because readers naturally interpreted repeated exposure as broad agreement. A prediction appearing on twenty websites looked stronger than one appearing on a single page, even if all twenty versions ultimately derived from one original message.

Modern research on misinformation demonstrates that repetition not only increases perceived accuracy but can also increase people's willingness to share a claim further, creating a feedback loop in which familiar statements spread more widely precisely because they already seem credible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010027723000550" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect The illusory truth effect leads to the spread of misinformation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The illusory truth effect leads to the spread of misinformation - ScienceDirect...</span></span></span>

For failed UFO predictions, this means that online popularity should not be confused with evidential strength. The internet greatly increased the visibility of prophetic claims, but it also made it easier for one unsupported prediction to masquerade as many independent confirmations.
