## Introduction

Researchers and reporters can change the behaviour of the small UFO groups they are trying to observe. The risk is greatest when a handful of committed believers are awaiting a failed prediction while numerous outsiders enter their meetings, ask for explanations, display apparent interest and offer the group an audience. In that setting, observation is not merely passive: newcomers may alter the group’s size, social confidence, sense of importance and decisions about publicity.



<figure class="article-media-figure article-media-figure-overview"><img src="{{ "/assets/images/failed_predictions_r_c5c2a1_researcher_influence_41da81-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Observer Effects" width="1024" height="559" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low"><figcaption class="article-media-caption"><span class="article-media-caption-label">Illustrative overview</span></figcaption></figure>

This problem now sits at the centre of the best-known UFO prophecy case. *When Prophecy Fails*, the influential 1956 study of [Dorothy Martin]({{ 'dorothy-martin/' | relative_url }})’s group, presented increased publicity after an unsuccessful flood-and-rescue prediction as evidence that disappointed believers sometimes defend their faith by recruiting others. Newly examined archival material, however, indicates that covert researchers were unusually numerous and sometimes actively intervened. The case therefore remains useful, but less as a clean demonstration of belief perseverance than as a warning about observer effects in tiny, high-commitment groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026by T Kelly · 2026 · Cited by 4 — Drawing on newly unsealed archival mat...</span></span></span>

## The observer effect problem

An [observer effect]({{ 'observer-effect/' | relative_url }}) occurs when people alter their conduct because they are being watched, questioned or joined by outsiders. This is sometimes called reactivity. It does not require participants to know that a formal study is taking place. They may simply respond to the apparent beliefs, confidence, curiosity or social status of the people around them.

* **Apparent growth:** researchers posing as believers can make a marginal movement look as though it is attracting converts.
* **Artificial agreement:** their attentive presence may reassure uncertain members that respected outsiders take the claims seriously.
* **Demand for performance:** reporters asking for statements can encourage leaders to issue clearer, more dramatic or more public declarations.
* **Changed group cohesion:** an influx of sympathetic-looking strangers can temporarily strengthen morale after a prediction fails.
* **Selective survival:** outsiders may spend most time with the most accessible or outspoken members, making them appear more representative than quieter people who withdraw.

The central difficulty is causal. When believers speak more confidently after a failed prediction, are they spontaneously resolving psychological conflict, responding to encouragement from supposed converts, defending themselves against hostile publicity, or performing for an audience that has suddenly made them newsworthy? The [Martin case]({{ 'martin-case/' | relative_url }}) does not provide a reliable way to isolate those explanations.

This does not prove that cognitive [dissonance]({{ 'dissonance/' | relative_url }})—the discomfort created by conflicting beliefs and evidence—never affects failed-prophecy groups. It means that this particular episode cannot be treated as an uncontaminated test of what believers would have done without researchers and journalists surrounding them. ## Researchers and reporters inside the group

The research team behind *When Prophecy Fails* used covert participant observation. Paid observers entered Martin’s circle while presenting themselves as people genuinely interested in flying saucers, spiritual messages and the predicted disaster. They attempted to attend important meetings, gain members’ trust and record events without revealing their research purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_Prophecy_Fails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When Prophecy Fails</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When Prophecy Fails</span></span></span>

Covert observation was intended to prevent believers from consciously performing for known psychologists. Yet concealment did not make the observers socially invisible. They still talked, listened, asked questions, received confidences and occupied seats in a very small room. Their professed interest was itself information: it suggested that outsiders found the group’s ideas plausible or important enough to pursue.

The problem was intensified by the group’s belief system. Martin’s followers reportedly allowed for the possibility that extraterrestrial beings could appear in human form. An unfamiliar but unusually receptive newcomer could therefore acquire significance far beyond that of an ordinary visitor. A researcher who seemed knowledgeable, confident or spiritually sympathetic might be interpreted as a verifier, messenger or specially guided participant rather than a neutral stranger.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_Prophecy_Fails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When Prophecy Fails</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When Prophecy Fails</span></span></span>

### From observation to intervention

Thomas Kelly’s archival reassessment, published online in November 2025 and in the *Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences*, makes a more serious allegation than simple reactivity. Drawing on previously unavailable research papers, Kelly argues that members of the team actively shaped episodes later presented as observations. His account describes [fabricated psychic]({{ 'fabricated-messages/' | relative_url }}) communications, covert manipulation and interference in a child-welfare investigation involving people connected to the group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">She had been influenced by...</span></span></span>

One especially important figure was co-author Henry Riecken. According to the archival evidence reported by Kelly and subsequently examined by *The New Yorker*, Riecken gained unusual standing among the believers and presented himself as an “[earthly verifier]({{ 'earthly-verifier/' | relative_url }})” who needed to question members on behalf of the supposed extraterrestrial authorities. The archives also indicate that, during a moment of doubt after an expected event failed, he expressed renewed confidence in ways that may have reassured Martin before she produced a message explaining that the group’s spiritual activity had saved the world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/2025-kelly.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gwern.net">[Gwern.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gwern.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span></span></span>

If that reconstruction is correct, the researchers did not merely witness believers rationalising failure. A trusted infiltrator helped create the immediate social conditions in which the rationalisation emerged. The published sequence—failed prediction, distress, new revelation, revived confidence—may still describe what happened chronologically, but it no longer establishes that the process arose independently from the believers themselves.

Kelly also disputes the famous claim that the group moved from relative secrecy to energetic proselytising only after the prophecy failed. Archival evidence indicates that Dorothy Martin and Charles Laughead had already pursued publicity: they had written for specialist publications, circulated material and attracted newspaper attention before the deadline. ### Reporters created a second audience

Journalistic attention introduced another form of intervention. Reporters repeatedly telephoned, sought interviews and pressed members to defend or clarify their prediction. On Christmas Eve, hundreds of spectators reportedly gathered outside Martin’s home while a further expected spacecraft arrival failed to occur. The group’s public conduct unfolded not in private isolation but amid reporters, police, neighbours and a crowd awaiting a spectacle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theatlantic.com/health/archive/2015/12/the-christmas-the-aliens-didnt-come/421122/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theatlantic.com">[The Atlantic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theatlantic.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Atlantic The Christmas the Aliens Didn&#x27;t Come</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Atlantic The Christmas the Aliens Didn&#x27;t Come</span></span></span>

[Media pressure]({{ 'media-pressure/' | relative_url }}) can push a small group in opposite directions. Members may retreat to avoid ridicule, or they may answer criticism with stronger declarations. Leaders may make new predictions because journalists demand a next step. Public scrutiny can also turn private uncertainty into a collective reputation problem: admitting error now means conceding defeat before neighbours, family members and a national audience.

For that reason, publicity after failure cannot automatically be interpreted as an attempt to recruit converts and reduce internal doubt. It may instead be defensive communication, crisis management or a response to outsiders who keep requesting statements. Earlier methodological critics had already warned that the press’s persistent questioning could have contributed to the very proselytising that the study treated as its principal finding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_Prophecy_Fails" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">When Prophecy Fails</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When Prophecy Fails</span></span></span>

## What can still be learned from the case?

The observer-effect criticism does not make every surviving detail worthless. Contemporary notes can still illuminate what particular people said, how explanations changed and how a prophecy crisis developed under intense public attention. The mistake is to move too quickly from that description to a universal psychological rule.

The strongest defensible conclusion is conditional: **in a tiny UFO prophecy group, failure is processed through the social environment present at the deadline**. That environment may include committed believers, wavering members, covert investigators, sympathetic newcomers, sceptical relatives, journalists and spectators. Each changes the pressures facing the others.

The case therefore supports several narrower insights:

**Small numbers magnify every intervention.** A few planted observers can alter the apparent balance between believers and doubters. Their enthusiasm may look like independent confirmation, particularly to members who do not know that the newcomers share an employer and research plan.

**Attention can be mistaken for validation.** Researchers and journalists may regard a prediction as implausible yet devote considerable effort to it because it offers a valuable story or study. Believers can interpret the same attention as evidence that their message is gaining recognition.

**Public failure differs from private failure.** A person reconsidering a belief quietly faces different costs from one being asked to explain it on camera, before strangers or in front of fellow adherents. Public questioning can produce defiance without indicating deeper private conviction.

**Researchers can become authorities inside fluid belief systems.** Where revelations are continually interpreted and newcomers may be assigned spiritual significance, an infiltrator’s remarks can be absorbed into the group’s decision-making. The distinction between observing doctrine and helping to create it can disappear rapidly.

**A compelling theory can shape the record.** The authors entered the field expecting certain highly committed believers to respond to disconfirmation through rationalisation and outreach. Kelly argues that contrary evidence—including earlier publicity and rapid abandonment by some members—was minimised or represented misleadingly. At a minimum, the episode shows why field notes, published narratives and theoretical conclusions must be distinguished from one another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jhbs.70043" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[wiley.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Online Library Debunking “When Prophecy Fails”</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wiley Online LibraryDebunking “When Prophecy Fails” - Kelly - 2026by T Kelly · 2026 · Cited by 4 — Drawing on newly unsealed archival mat...</span></span></span>

## How future case studies can reduce distortion

Research into contemporary UFO prophecy groups remains possible, but the design must recognise that access itself can change the phenomenon. Modern ethical guidance generally treats informed consent as the norm and covert or deceptive observation as an exceptional method requiring strong justification and independent review. The British Sociological Association advises that undisclosed observation in non-public settings or manipulation without participants’ knowledge should be used only when essential information cannot be obtained through other methods. UK Research and Innovation similarly says covert research should be reserved for cases where no other approach is possible, it is crucial to the design, or overt observation would substantially alter what is being studied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britsoc.co.uk/equality/issues-for-bsa-members/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britsoc.co.uk">[British Sociological Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britsoc.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britsoc.co.uk.</span></span></span>

A stronger UFO-prophecy study would use several safeguards.

1. **Keep observers proportionate to the group.** One or two researchers should not become a major fraction of active attendance. When continuous coverage is impossible without flooding the group with observers, incomplete observation is less damaging than artificial membership.
2. **Separate observation from participation.** Researchers should not endorse predictions, invent revelations, claim special spiritual authority or advise leaders how to respond when a deadline fails. Any unavoidable interaction should be logged as a possible intervention rather than silently treated as part of the group’s natural behaviour.
3. **Record the surrounding audience.** Field notes should distinguish members, occasional visitors, researchers, journalists, spectators and people attracted by media coverage. Statements such as “the group became more active” are meaningless unless the changing composition of the group is documented.
4. **Establish the pre-failure baseline independently.** Researchers should collect dated newsletters, letters, press releases, recordings and newspaper coverage from before the prediction. That reduces the risk of misclassifying longstanding outreach as a sudden reaction to failure.
5. **Track individual outcomes, not just the loudest survivors.** A group can appear more committed because doubters leave while a vocal minority remains. Studies should document withdrawal, silence, private doubt and reduced participation alongside public declarations of faith.
6. **Preserve contradictory evidence.** Research archives should retain field notes, coding decisions and accounts that challenge the final interpretation. Where privacy permits, later scholars should be able to test whether the published narrative omitted disconfirming material.
7. **Treat media exposure as a variable.** Researchers should document the timing and volume of press contact, as well as whether public statements were volunteered or requested. A prediction failure watched by hundreds of spectators is not comparable to one discussed privately among five adherents.

## The lasting lesson of the watched believers

The question is not whether Dorothy Martin’s flood and flying-saucer rescue prediction failed; it plainly did. The unresolved issue is how her followers would have responded had they not been surrounded by covert investigators, reporters and spectators.

The classic account offered a memorable sequence in which failed prophecy produced stronger conviction and public evangelism. The archival challenge shows that some of the apparent converts were researchers, some influential interactions were interventions, publicity existed before the failure, and the group largely dispersed soon afterwards. [Brill+2Wiley Online Library](https://brill.com/display/book/edcoll/9789004222687/B9789004222687-s006.pdf?srsltid=AfmBOorRgugdsX3Ujn46Yw_YWJu4Msd1qHZhVPM_H9ZlMG9UnHRyIY5z)

For the study of failed UFO predictions, this changes the emphasis. Researchers should not assume that a small group’s post-failure behaviour reveals a self-contained psychological mechanism. It may instead reveal a temporary system formed by believers and the people watching them. In groups this small, the observers are not outside the story. They can become part of the evidence they later claim to explain.
